Understanding the Federal Budget Process in Nigeria

By Editorial Team — Published: 2026-08-05 — Updated: 2026-09-22

The federal budget is the federal government's annual plan and legal framework for authorised public expenditure and expected revenue. Understanding the cycle helps citizens distinguish between a proposed allocation, an approved appropriation, money released for implementation, actual expenditure and audited results.

Important: Budget figures can change through appropriation amendments, supplementary appropriations and implementation decisions. When discussing a current figure, check the latest Budget Office of the Federation document rather than relying on an older summary.

1. The medium-term framework comes first

Federal annual budgeting is linked to the Medium-Term Expenditure Framework and Fiscal Strategy Paper (MTEF/FSP). The Budget Office explains that the framework connects government policies, plans and budgets over the medium term. The Budget Office published the 2026–2028 MTEF/FSP on 17 December 2025.

This stage matters because the medium-term framework establishes fiscal assumptions and expenditure considerations that inform the annual budget. It is therefore useful to read the MTEF/FSP alongside the annual Appropriation documents rather than treating the annual budget as an isolated document.

2. Ministries and agencies prepare estimates

Federal ministries, departments and agencies prepare budget estimates within the government's budget framework and instructions. The Budget Office uses Budget Call Circulars to communicate requirements for preparing estimates; its resource library currently includes 2026 call-circular documents.

The Budget Office also states that it coordinates Medium Term Sector Strategies across federal MDAs and links those strategies to annual budget estimates through Budget Call Circulars.

3. The executive consolidates the proposal

Estimates from MDAs are consolidated into the federal budget proposal. The executive considers the proposed revenue and expenditure framework before the Appropriation Bill is presented to the National Assembly.

For example, the Budget Office published details of the 2026 Appropriation Bill on 8 January 2026. It subsequently published details of the 2026 Appropriation Act on 10 August 2026. These separate documents illustrate why citizens should identify whether they are reading a proposal or the enacted appropriation.

4. The National Assembly scrutinises the budget

The National Assembly has a constitutional role in considering appropriation. Its published budget-cycle material describes stages including MTEF approval, presentation of the budget, general debate, committee consideration, reporting and passage. Appropriation committees and relevant standing committees examine the estimates, including through budget-defence processes and hearings.

The legislative stage is important because the executive proposal and the final appropriation are not necessarily identical documents. When checking an allocation, use the enacted Appropriation Act as the reference for what was ultimately appropriated, while retaining the proposal for comparison.

5. Assent and the Appropriation Act

After legislative passage, the Appropriation Bill proceeds through the constitutional process for becoming an appropriation law. The Budget Office's current 2026 records distinguish the Appropriation Bill from the later Appropriation Act. The 2026 appropriation document states that the appropriation applies for the 2026 financial year.

6. Implementation is different from appropriation

An appropriation authorises spending; it does not mean that the entire amount has already been spent. During implementation, government institutions operate within financial, procurement and release procedures, and actual spending can differ from the amount originally appropriated.

The Budget Office publishes Budget Implementation Reports. Its current website includes quarterly and consolidated implementation reports as well as other budget documents.

7. Procurement and projects

Where an appropriation funds a project or procurement activity, citizens should not assume that an appropriation alone proves that a contract was awarded or that work was completed. Project monitoring requires looking beyond the budget line to relevant procurement information, implementation reports and, where available, evidence of physical progress.

8. Audit and legislative oversight

Public spending is also subject to audit and oversight. The Office of the Auditor-General for the Federation has constitutional responsibilities concerning the audit of the public accounts of the Federation and reporting to the National Assembly. Its published materials also describe checks involving federal corporations, commissions and agencies.

The National Assembly's oversight structures, including its Public Accounts Committee, can consider audit findings and other financial accountability matters. This is part of the accountability stage after expenditure has occurred.

9. How citizens can follow one budget item

  1. Identify the institution: note the ministry, department or agency responsible.
  2. Find the budget line: use the relevant Budget Office appropriation document and record the programme, project or expenditure head.
  3. Check the status: determine whether you are looking at a proposal, enacted appropriation, release, implementation report or audit finding.
  4. Check procurement: where relevant, look for the responsible institution's procurement or contract information.
  5. Check implementation: compare reported expenditure or progress with the approved allocation.
  6. Check audit information: where an audit report covers the institution or expenditure, read the relevant finding and response.
  7. Keep the documents: record document titles, dates and page numbers so that another reader can reproduce the check.

10. Useful terms

  • MTEF/FSP: the medium-term framework connecting fiscal strategy, policies, plans and expenditure.
  • Budget Call Circular: instructions and requirements used to guide MDAs in preparing budget estimates.
  • Appropriation Bill: the proposed legislation setting out requested expenditure authority before enactment.
  • Appropriation Act: the enacted appropriation providing legal authority for expenditure for the relevant period.
  • Budget implementation: the process of carrying out the expenditure and programmes authorised in the budget, subject to applicable controls.
  • Audit: examination and reporting on public financial records and related matters within the Auditor-General's mandate.
